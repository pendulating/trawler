Abstract

The advancement of machine learning systems depends on large-scale, high-quality datasets. However, corpora drawn from user-generated and proprietary domains are often laden with sensitive information, posing significant privacy, security, and compliance risks. Conventional anonymization methods, which focus on removing explicit identifiers, can degrade downstream performance and leave the more nuanced challenge of implicit privacy leakage unresolved. This form of leakage allows for sensitive attributes such as author identity, demographics, or personality to be inferred from writing style alone. To address this, we present a privacy-preserving text rewriting framework based on guided reinforcement learning. Our approach features a composite reward function that operates over disentangled semantic and stylistic representations to preserve utility while enforcing style convergence and author anonymity. Empirical validation demonstrates substantial improvements on implicit privacy metrics without sacrificing semantic fidelity, yielding a scalable, model-agnostic solution for privacy-preserving data generation in the age of Large Language Models.

1 Introduction

The rapid growth of user-generated text and the pervasive use of machine learning to process it have elevated privacy protection from a technical concern to a socio-technical imperative. Textual corpora ranging from electronic health records and legal filings to social media and product reviews offer substantial value for research, analytics, and service optimization. Yet they also embed sensitive personal information that can be misused for targeted manipulation or discrimination. Regulations such as the GDPR [18] require rigorous safeguards, making effective anonymization a practical necessity. Despite substantial efforts to protect user privacy, two major challenges remain.

Most existing studies focus on protecting explicit privacy through the anonymization of Personally Identifiable Information (PII), while overlooking the subtler threat of implicit privacy leakage[26]. Adversaries can infer sensitive attributes (e.g., gender, age, religion, interests) from linguistic signals such as topic choices, lexical preferences, or discourse structure [19]. For example, references to culturally specific holidays can reveal religious affiliation. Moreover, Large Language Models (LLMs) can exacerbate this risk by acting as effective “deanonymizers” and inferring identities from contextual information that remains after standard anonymization. These inferred attributes and their combinations can substantially increase the likelihood of re-identification [27], thereby exposing an author’s identity. The risk can be further compounded by stylometric leakage. An author’s characteristic “fingerprint” defined by word choice, sentence structure, and punctuation, often remains stable even after PII is scrubbed, enabling high-accuracy re-identification through stylometry [28]. Authors who are stylistic or lexical outliers (i.e., exhibit statistically unusual patterns) are especially vulnerable to both attribute inference and re-identification attacks. Another challenge is the inherent privacy–utility trade-off. Aggressive sanitization can impair readability and downstream performance, undermining data value. Techniques such as Differential Privacy (DP), explored for text rewriting and privacy-preserving data generation or analysis, offer formal, provable guarantees. However, strong protection (small $\epsilon$) typically harms fluency and task performance; applying DP effectively to complex, unstructured text while maintaining high utility is difficult and often requires intricate model design and careful parameter tuning. Moreover, DP provides population-level probabilistic guarantees rather than per-instance redaction, so concrete PII and implicit cues (e.g., style, dialect) may persist.
Leveraging advances in LLMs that enable the detection and transformation of sensitive information, LLM-based rewriting offers a promising approach to replacing sensitive content with semantically equivalent text [16], and parallel research has achieved success in altering stylistic features of text [9]. However, these approaches do not significantly address implicit privacy risks. Furthermore, they lack a tunable mechanism to optimize the critical tradeoff between privacy and utility.

This work aims to transform text so that individuals cannot be re-identified while preserving utility for downstream tasks. Because of the lightweight design of the framework, it can be deployed on edge devices to locally rewrite sensitive text before data sharing. We address these gaps with a reinforcement-learning (RL) text-rewriting framework that fine-tunes an LLM to navigate the privacy–utility frontier explicitly. For each input, the model generates multiple diverse paraphrases and optimizes them with a composite reward that integrates signals for implicit privacy preservation (stylometric obfuscation, attribute suppression) and utility preservation (semantic fidelity). This yields an adaptive policy that reduces implicit leakage while maintaining meaning and usability. Our primary contributions are as follows:

- We introduce multi-faceted metrics for implicit privacy risk that jointly capture author identifiability (e.g., entropy over author classifiers) and non-semantic style-embedding structure, providing population-level indicators of implicit leakage.

- We design an LLM-based RL framework with a composite reward that operationalizes the privacy–utility trade-off, directly minimizing authorial identifiability while preserving semantic content through paraphrase diversity and fidelity controls.

2 Related Work

Early efforts of authorship obfuscation (AO) relied on simple rule-based edits, such as synonym substitution and contraction expansion [11, 21], which often compromised grammatical fluency and semantic accuracy. A subsequent line of work framed AO as an adversarial attack against authorship attribution (AA) models, employing gradient-based [6] or black-box perturbations [8] to mislead classifiers. However, these adversarial edits frequently degraded text readability [4]. Deep generative models, including VAEs and GANs [17, 22], and methods based on targeted linguistic feature manipulation [25] represented a more sophisticated approach, aiming to disentangle style from content. Despite their advances, these techniques, along with back-translation [1, 5] and style-axis manipulation [7], often struggled with semantic drift or left residual author signals in the text.

The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has introduced new privacy challenges while also providing powerful new tools for mitigation risks [2, 23]. One major direction involves incorporating formal privacy guarantees, such as training models with differential privacy (DP) [15] or adding DP-noise during the decoding process [10, 14, 24], though these methods often harm text fidelity. Another direction focuses on practical text rewriting. This includes...
frameworks like DIPPER [12], which separates utility and privacy controls for tunable rewriting, and prompt sanitizers like Casper [3], which suppress PII before model inference. Complementing these methods, recent findings show that repeated LLM paraphrasing induces a natural convergence of writing styles [16], motivating the development of more advanced anonymization strategies that leverage this phenomenon.

These methods lack the precision to surgically remove an author’s implicit stylistic fingerprint. Rule-based and adversarial methods achieve obfuscation at the cost of text quality, while generative and DP-based approaches either require costly model retraining or degrade semantic fidelity. Furthermore, existing LLM solutions lack a tunable mechanism to manage the privacy-utility tradeoff. Our work addresses this gap by directly optimizing for both stylometric anonymity and semantic preservation, navigating the privacy-utility frontier more effectively than previous approaches.

3 RL Framework for Implicit Privacy Preserving

3.1 Defining and Measuring Implicit Privacy

A system preserves implicit privacy if sensitive information not explicitly disclosed by a user cannot be reliably inferred from its exposed artifacts. Formally, let $S$ be a sensitive attribute, $y$ be the rewritten exposed artifact of a mechanism $M$, and $\mathcal{A}$ be a class of adversaries producing predictions $A(y)$ for $S$. We say $M$ preserves $(\delta, \mathcal{A})$-implicit privacy if:

$$\sup_{A \in \mathcal{A}} \left( \Pr[A(y) = S] - \max_{s \in S} \Pr[S = s] \right) \leq \delta.$$  

Here, $\max_s \Pr[S = s]$ represents the baseline success rate of an adversary who always guesses the most frequent value of $S$ (the majority class). The term within the supremum is the adversary’s advantage over this baseline. The inequality requires that no adversary in $\mathcal{A}$ can improve upon this simple baseline by more than a small margin $\delta$.

We quantify implicit privacy across three distinct threat models, each corresponding to a significant real-world risk. For each, we define the adversary’s task, their capabilities ($\mathcal{A}$), and their advantage as per Equation (1).

- **Attribute Inference** This threat model measures the risk of an adversary inferring undisclosed user attributes $S_{att}$ (e.g., age, gender, location) from the rewritten artifacts $y$. The adversary class, $\mathcal{A}_{att}$, comprises a suite of models with increasing complexity, ranging from linear probes to fine-tuned transformer networks. The advantage is the adversary’s accuracy improvement over a majority-class baseline:

$$\text{Adv}_{att} = \max_{A \in \mathcal{A}_{att}} \left( \text{Acc}(A; y, S_{att}) - \max_{k \leq K} \pi_k \right) \leq \delta_{att}$$  

- **Authorship Identification (Closed-Set)** This threat model evaluates the risk of de-anonymizing an artifact’s author from a known, pre-defined set of candidates $\mathcal{H}$. The adversary class, $\mathcal{A}_{auth}$, includes robust metric-learning encoders and fine-tuned classifiers operating under fixed computational budgets. The advantage is the excess Top-$k$ accuracy (where $k = 1$ typically refers to direct accuracy) over random guessing from the set of authors:

$$\text{Adv}_{closed}^{\text{auth}} = \max_{A \in \mathcal{A}_{auth}} \left( \text{Top-1}(A) - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}|} \right) \leq \delta_{auth}$$  

- **Authorship Attribution (Open-Set)** This model addresses the heightened risk faced by stylistic outliers, where a user’s unique “stylistic fingerprint” makes them identifiable even in a large, open set. While most users’ styles tend to cluster, an outlier’s style embedding is distinctly isolated, creating a significant vulnerability. The adversary’s task is to match an anonymous query $y$ (derived from an outlier’s original text $x$) to the same author’s work within a large gallery $\mathcal{G}$. Adversaries ($\mathcal{A}_{ret}$) employ nearest-neighbor search (e.g., kNN/ANN) over document embeddings. The advantage is the retrieval hit rate over random chance:

$$\text{Adv}_{ret} = \max_{A \in \mathcal{A}_{ret}} \left( \text{Hit}@k - \frac{k}{|\mathcal{G}|} \right) \leq \delta_{ret}$$  

3.2 RL-guided Privacy-Preserving Text Rewriting

To systematically protect against the implicit privacy risks defined above, we propose a novel method for privacy-preserving text rewriting. We frame this as a Reinforcement Learning (RL) problem where a generator model is trained to balance the competing goals of utility and privacy. The core of our method is a composite reward function that intelligently guides the generator, enabled by a specialized technique for measuring authorial style.

3.2.1 Learning Disentangled Representations. Our framework’s guidance system relies on separate encoders to map text into distinct semantic and stylistic subspaces. This disentanglement is crucial for independently controlling meaning and style.

- **Semantic Subspace ($f_{\text{sem}}$):** To capture meaning, we utilize embeddings from robust pre-trained encoders, such as RoBERTa [13]. These models are renowned for their sensitivity to semantic content and their relative robustness to surface-level stylistic variations, making them ideal for assessing utility.

- **Stylistic Subspace ($f_{\text{style}}$):** To explicitly capture an author’s unique “fingerprint” and stylistic traits, we learn a specialized, privacy-aware style encoder. This involves training an MLP head on top of a RoBERTa encoder, using an author classification task as the supervisory signal. This forces the resulting style embeddings, $f_{\text{style}}(\cdot)$, to be highly discriminative of author identity.

As shown in Figure 2, this technique effectively separates authors into distinct clusters in the stylistic subspace, validating that $f_{\text{style}}$ captures re-identifiable signals and is thus a suitable foundation for our privacy-focused rewards.

3.2.2 Stylistic Outlier Detection. We represent each input text $x$ as a high-dimensional vector $v_x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ using the learned style encoder from Section 3.2.1 to capture stylistic features like tone, syntax, and expression. Given a reference corpus of prior texts $X = \{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ with corresponding style embeddings $V = \{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$, we quantify the stylistic uniqueness of a new input $x$ by computing its average cosine distance to the corpus:

$$\tilde{d}_x = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (1 - \cos(v_x, v_i)),$$
where $\cos(\cdot, \cdot)$ denotes the cosine similarity. We define an adaptive outlier threshold $\tau$ based on the empirical distribution of these distances:

$$\tau = \mu + \lambda \cdot \sigma,$$

(6)

where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation of the average distances $\{\bar{d}_i\}_{i=1}^n$ across the reference corpus $X$, and $\lambda$ is a tunable hyperparameter that controls the sensitivity of the detector.

If $d_x > \tau$, the input is flagged as a **stylistic outlier**. When an outlier is detected, our system adaptively modifies the generation policy by conditioning the LLM via a rewriting prompt that emphasizes generality, neutrality, and a formal tone, thereby discouraging the model from replicating the unique and potentially identifying stylistic traits. Furthermore, the detection of an outlier directly influences the Style Convergence reward (Section 3.2.3).

### 3.2.3 The Composite Reward Function

The composite reward function $R(y)$ is the heart of our guidance mechanism. It scores a candidate text $y$ based on a weighted sum of six components that balance utility, fluency, and multi-faceted privacy goals.

#### Utility Signals

These rewards ensure the output is high-quality and useful:

- **Semantic Fidelity ($r_{\text{sem}}$):** High BERTScore between the original ($x$) and rewritten text ($y$).
- **Length Consistency ($r_{\text{length}}$):** A Gaussian penalty for significant length deviation from the original text.

#### Privacy Signals

These rewards directly mitigate the privacy risks we identified:

- **Entity Control ($r_{\text{entity}}$):** A penalty for copying named entities from the source. This addresses explicit privacy by discouraging memorization of sensitive data.
- **Style Convergence ($r_{\text{converge}}$):** To address implicit privacy, this reward uses our learned style embedding $f_{\text{style}}$ to guide the output ($y$) towards a neutral, population-average style ($\mu_{\text{style}}$), explicitly penalizing stylistic uniqueness. We define the $\mu_{\text{style}}$ as the global mean in style space. The reward employs a conditional scoring mechanism based on whether the original input text $x$ belongs to a set of identified stylistic outliers ($O$). The reward is defined as:

$$r_{\text{converge}}(y) = \begin{cases} -d_{\text{style}}(y, \mu_{\text{style}}), & \text{if original input } x \in O \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

(7)

Here, $d_{\text{style}}(y, \mu_{\text{style}})$ represents the distance between the style embedding of the rewritten output $y$ and the population-average style $\mu_{\text{style}}$.

- **Author Anonymity ($r_{\text{entropy}}$):** To further enhance implicit privacy, this reward encourages ambiguity by maximizing the predictive entropy of a pre-trained author classifier. This discourages the output from strongly identifying with any single author.

$$r_{\text{entropy}}(y) = \frac{H(p_\phi(\cdot|y))}{\log(n_{\text{classes}})},$$

$$H(p) = -\sum_a p(a|y) \log p(a|y).$$

(8)

Here, $n_{\text{classes}}$ is the number of authors used to train the author classifier, providing a normalization factor.

#### Formal Composition

The total reward for a candidate rewrite $y$ is the weighted sum $R(y) = \sum_{i=1}^{6} \lambda_i \cdot r_i(y)$, where the weights $\lambda_i$ are tunable hyperparameters that control the overall privacy-utility trade-off.

### 3.2.4 RL Training and Optimization

The overall training and optimization flow is shown in Figure 3. The training loop begins with Prompt Construction phase, where each input text is analyzed for explicit PII and stylistic uniqueness to create a tailored prompt. Based on this prompt, our LoRA-adapted Llama-3.2-3B model generates multiple candidate rewrites. These candidates are then scored by the composite reward function, which accounts for utility, entity control, and implicit privacy signals like style convergence and author anonymity. Finally, the GRPO algorithm uses these scores to compute a policy gradient and update the model’s LoRA weights. This end-to-end process iteratively refines the generator to produce outputs that are resilient to the implicit privacy threats defined in this work.
4 Experimental Evaluation

4.1 Experimental Setup

4.1.1 Datasets. We conduct our evaluation on a diverse set of corpora. We use three benchmarks with rich authorial signals: Yelp, Twitter, and IMDb. To specifically measure implicit attribute leakage, we use the synthetic SynthPAI corpus [28]. To assess utility generalization on downstream tasks, we use AG News [29], ECInstruct [20], and TrustPilot.

4.1.2 Compared Methods. We benchmark our approach against four methods: (1) Presidio, a rule-based entity redactor; (2) StyleRemix [7], which modifies syntax and vocabulary to mask authorial style; (3) DIPPER [12], a controllable paraphrasing model that disentangles privacy and utility signals; and (4) DP-MLM [15], a differentially private masked-language-model generator.

4.1.3 Implementation Details. Our generator is a 4-bit quantized Llama-3.2-3B model configured with rank-32 LoRA adapters. We train the model for ten epochs using the GRPO framework, guided by our composite reward function. For optimization, we use adamw_torch_fused with a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-5}$, an effective batch size of 8, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.99$, and a weight decay of 0.1. A cosine learning rate scheduler with a 10% warmup ratio and gradient clipping (max norm 0.1) are also applied. For downstream utility evaluation, we fine-tune a separate BERT-base-uncased classifier on each rewritten dataset.

4.2 Evaluation Metrics

We assess all methods on utility and a multi-faceted view of privacy, separated into explicit and implicit risks.

4.2.1 Utility ($\uparrow$). We evaluate semantic fidelity using BERTScore and downstream task performance with Sentiment/Topic F1 scores.

4.2.2 Explicit Privacy ($\downarrow$). This metric measures the leakage of directly observable sensitive tokens. Letting $E(x)$ be the set of PII entities in the original text and $E(y)$ for the rewrite text, the metric is:

$$\text{Entity Match} = \frac{|E(x) \cap E(y)|}{|E(x)|}. \quad (9)$$

4.2.3 Implicit Privacy ($\downarrow$). These metrics directly correspond to the implicit threat models defined in our framework, quantifying an adversary’s ability to infer hidden information:

- **Author/Gender F1**: Measures the success of an adversary on the Attribute Inference task. A low F1 score indicates that stylistic cues related to identity and demographics have been successfully obfuscated.
- **PEI**: Quantifies traceability as the probability that an adversary recovers the original $x$ from only the rewrite $y$ via embedding-similarity retrieval $R(y, C)$. A low PEI shows the rewrite is well-hidden and not stylistically isolated.

$$\text{PEI} = P(R(y, C) = x). \quad (10)$$

- **Outlier Similarity**: Directly measures resilience to Open-Set Authorship Attribution. It quantifies similarity to known stylistic outliers $O$. A low score proves the model avoids replicating risky, identifiable writing styles.

$$\text{Outlier Sim} = \max_{v_o \in O} \frac{v_y \cdot v_o}{\|v_y\|\|v_o\|}. \quad (11)$$

4.3 Quantitative Analysis

Our quantitative evaluation confirms that our framework successfully navigates the core challenges of text anonymization. The results demonstrate two key findings: 1) our method provides a new state-of-the-art in mitigating the nuanced risks of implicit privacy, and 2) it achieves this while maintaining high utility, offering a superior privacy-utility tradeoff compared to existing baselines.

4.3.1 Implicit Privacy Protection. A core achievement of our work is the significant reduction of implicit privacy risks. As shown in Table 1, our method consistently provides the strongest protections across three implicit threat models: for attribute inference, we obtain the lowest Gender F1 (0.4231), indicating effective obfuscation of subtle demographic cues; for authorship analysis (closed-set identification), we reduce the Author-ID F1 on Yelp to a state-of-the-art 0.2197, surpassing all baselines; and for anonymity and traceability (open-set attribution and tracing to training sets), we achieve the lowest Outlier Similarity together with lowest PEI scores across all datasets, demonstrating neutralization of uniquely identifiable styles and strong protection against being traced or identified as part of a training set.

To further prove our model’s performance on the implicit privacy challenges, we evaluated it on the SynthPAI corpus. As shown in Table 2, we reduce the identifiability of Age and Education with a remarkable 70% F1 score reduction compared to the next-best baseline. This result confirms that our methodology is exceptionally effective at neutralizing the subtle linguistic patterns that reveal sensitive, unstated personal information.

4.3.2 Privacy-Utility Tradeoff. Our method achieved high performance in privacy protection without the significant utility degradation. While approaches like Presidio achieve high utility (e.g., 0.9866 BERTScore), they offer almost no protection against implicit threats (e.g., 0.8041 Author-ID F1). Conversely, methods like DP-MLM provide some privacy but at a steep cost to semantic fidelity. We deliver competitive BERTScore and top-tier downstream Sentiment F1 scores, demonstrating that our framework successfully resolves the tension between robust privacy and real-world functionality.

To confirm that our model’s high utility is not confined to a single task type, we evaluated its performance on three utility-only corpora with distinct formats: topic classification, instruction-following, and multi-class sentiment analysis. As shown in Table 3, our framework demonstrates robust and generalizable utility. It achieves the best performance on ECInstruct (instruction following) and is highly competitive on AG News and TReview, ranking second only to the non-private Presidio baseline. These results verify that our privacy-preserving training does not compromise the model’s fundamental language capabilities, ensuring it remains effective across a wide range of real-world applications beyond the primary privacy benchmarks.

5 Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced a reinforcement learning framework for privacy-preserving text rewriting that mitigates both explicit and implicit privacy risks. Leveraging a LoRA-adapted Llama-3.2-3B and implicit privacy-aware rewards, our method achieves strong implicit privacy anonymization while preserving semantic fidelity and task utility. Future work includes exploring dynamic, user-adaptive privacy–utility trade-offs, extending the framework to multilingual and low-resource settings.
